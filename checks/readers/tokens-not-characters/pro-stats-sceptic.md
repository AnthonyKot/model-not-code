### 3. What didn't — where I got lost, doubted the author, felt talked down to, or could not follow the mechanism. Quote the spot. If the worked example's numbers did not come out for me, say what I got.
I doubted the author when they claimed: "The merge count is the only knob." That is mathematically false. The pre-tokenization splitting rule (which they casually mention earlier as "Split the training corpus into words") is a massive configuration knob that heavily dictates boundary conditions. I also caught a discrepancy in the code. The comment claims `# ties broken alphabetically`, but `best = max(pairs, key=lambda p: (pairs[p], p))` actually selects the *last* alphabetical pair in a tie. Since `max()` seeks the highest value, comparing `('a','b')` and `('c','d')` yields `('c','d')`. It is deterministic, but misleadingly labeled. Finally, while the arithmetic in the worked example holds up, the numbers are blatantly contrived to produce exactly `sun` and `sung` in three perfectly clean steps. It is a curated demonstration, not a robust test of the algorithm’s behavior on messy distributions.

### 5. What changed between read one and read two.
On the first read, I accepted the overarching narrative that tokenization is the sole reason models fail at counting letters. On the second read, the statistical caveat near the end stood out: "It does not prove that a model cannot count them, only that it must do so from training text rather than from the word in front of it". This is a crucial, rigorous distinction. The model *does* estimate conditional probabilities of letters given the token ID based on corpus co-occurrence; tokenization just makes that signal incredibly sparse. My initial assumption that the author was hand-waving away the model's actual probabilistic capacity was corrected by spotting this precise qualifier.

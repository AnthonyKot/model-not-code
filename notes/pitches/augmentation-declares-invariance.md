# augmentation-declares-invariance — pitches (2026-09-12)

**Working title:** Augmentation Is Telling the Model What Does Not Matter
**Mechanism (catalog):** Label-preserving transforms enlarge the training distribution; mixup and cutmix mix the labels too.

## Pitch A — a transform is a claim, and a wrong claim costs accuracy

You added the standard augmentation stack — rotate, flip, contrast — and validation accuracy dropped, which is the opposite of what the tutorial promised, because one of those transforms asserted an invariance your labels do not have. The essay explains augmentation as a set of claims of the form "the label does not depend on this": each transform T declares that f(T(x)) should equal f(x), the training set becomes every reachable variant of every image, and a transform that is false for your labels (or that produces inputs the test set never contains) spends the model's capacity learning a function nobody will call; the lecture's reported case — faces rotated up to a quarter turn, accuracy falling from 75% to 54%, recovering to about 78% once the rotation was limited to a few degrees — is the shape of the failure. The worked example is arithmetic on the rotation parameter as the framework defines it (a factor is a fraction of a full turn, so 0.25 is 90° and 0.025 is 9°) followed by an invented count: 1,000 training images, three transforms each applied with probability 0.5, twenty epochs — the reader computes that an untouched original appears with probability 0.125 per presentation, so of 20,000 presentations about 2,500 are originals and 17,500 are variants, which is why a single wrong transform touches most of what the model sees. The exercise is a paper audit: for four tasks (faces, cell microscopy, handwritten digits, road signs) and eight transforms (horizontal flip, vertical flip, 180° rotation, small rotation, crop, brightness, grid shuffle, cutout), mark each cell "label-preserving and seen at test time", "label-preserving but never seen at test time" or "changes the label"; the expected result has 180° rotation and vertical flip failing for faces and for the digits 6 and 9, horizontal flip failing for signs with text, and all rotations passing for cells, with at least two cells the reader cannot fill without looking at the data — which is the point.

Draws on: course-4735368/11-04 (rotation factor as a fraction of a full turn; reported 75% → 54% with 90° rotations and ≈78% after limiting to 9°; unusual angles absent from the validation set), course-4735368/08-05 (choosing transforms by whether they remove the information that separates the classes; rotation safe for cell images, cropping avoided; per-transform probability p). Supporting book: Géron, *Hands-On Machine Learning* (PyTorch edition), data augmentation section.

## Pitch B — augmentation stops memorisation by never showing the same input twice

Your training accuracy is 99% and your validation accuracy is 77%, and adding more epochs widens the gap, because a fixed training set of a few thousand images can be memorised and the model is doing exactly that. The essay explains augmentation as sampling from an orbit: instead of a fixed set of N images, each epoch draws a fresh variant of each image, so the model cannot fit pixel patterns that will not recur and is pushed toward whatever the variants share — with the lecture's reported before-and-after (training accuracy near 99% against validation near 77% without augmentation; training about 86% against about 80% with rotation limits and cutmix) as the signature of the change, and the per-transform probability p as the knob that sets how much of each epoch is new. The worked example (invented) is a dataset of 500 images with a horizontal flip at p = 0.5 and a vertical flip at p = 0.5: the reader computes that each image has four discrete variants, that a given variant recurs on average every four epochs, and that over 40 epochs the model sees each variant about ten times — still memorisable — whereas adding a continuous rotation drawn uniformly from −9° to +9° makes every presentation distinct, so discrete flips alone do not stop memorisation and a continuous transform does; then the lecturer's reported heuristic (lower p for large datasets, higher for small) is read as "how many of the N × epochs presentations should be originals". The exercise uses numpy only: build 100 random 8×8 arrays as stand-in images, apply the two flips with p = 0.5 for 40 epochs, and count distinct arrays seen per image; the expected result is at most four per image with flips alone, and 40 per image once a small random rotation (or a random one-pixel shift) is added, which the reader then relates to the train/validation gap in their own curves.

Draws on: course-4735368/11-04 (augmentation as diversity against overfitting; reported training and validation accuracies before and after; training and validation curves moving together after augmentation), course-4735368/08-05 (probability p per transform, the reported example of 3,000 of 10,000 images augmented at p = 0.3, and the reported p heuristics for large versus small datasets; training accuracy no longer reaching 99% after augmentation). Supporting book: Géron, *Hands-On Machine Learning* (PyTorch edition).

## Pitch C — mixup changes the label, and the loss follows

You copied a mixup recipe, training loss rose and stayed high, and you concluded the model was broken, when the loss was doing exactly what a mixed label asks: the target is no longer one-hot, so a perfect classifier is now the wrong answer. The essay explains what mixup and cutmix declare — that the model's output should move linearly between two examples as the input does — by following one mixed pair through the arithmetic: x' = λx₁ + (1 − λ)x₂ with the same λ applied to the labels, cross-entropy against a soft target, and λ drawn from a Beta(α, α) whose shape at the paper's α (reported in the lecture as 0.2) puts most draws near 0 or 1, so most mixed images are mostly one image; cutmix pastes a box instead of blending and sets λ to the fraction of area kept. The worked example (invented) is two one-hot labels [1, 0] and [0, 1] with λ = 0.3, giving the target [0.3, 0.7]: the reader computes the cross-entropy of three candidate outputs — [0.3, 0.7], [0.5, 0.5] and [0, 1] — and finds the minimum is at the mixed target itself (about 0.61 nats), so a loss that "will not go to zero" is the expected floor and not a bug; then cutmix with a 32 × 32 patch pasted into a 128 × 128 image gives λ = 1 − 1024/16384 = 0.9375, and the target [0.9375, 0.0625]. The exercise is numpy only: draw 10,000 values from Beta(0.2, 0.2) and from Beta(1, 1), and report the fraction with 0.2 < λ < 0.8; the expected result is a small fraction at α = 0.2 (most draws near the ends) and 60% at α = 1 (uniform), which tells the reader what changing α actually changes and why the lecture's reported poor result on one dataset is a mismatch to investigate rather than a rule.

Draws on: course-4735368/08-03 (mixup formula for inputs and labels; λ from a Beta with α reported as 0.2 from the paper; the mixed label belonging to neither class; the reported poor result on the cell dataset), course-4735368/11-04 (cutmix as combining two images; reported training and validation accuracy with cutmix). Supporting book: Géron, *Hands-On Machine Learning* (PyTorch edition); the cutmix and mixup papers for the λ definitions if the essay states them as more than examples.

# autoencoder-learns-normal — pitches (2026-09-13)

**Working title:** An Autoencoder Learns “Normal”; Anomaly Is Reconstruction Error
**Mechanism (catalog):** A bottleneck forces reconstruction of what is common; reconstruction error is a label-free score, and a quantile threshold fixes the flag rate, not the anomaly rate.
**Placement:** Part III, after `labels-arrive-later`, as the detector that needs no labels.

## Pitch A — the bottleneck is a line, and the score is the distance to it

You have months of sensor rows and not one labelled failure, so none of the classifiers in the book so far can be trained, and the usual fallback — flag whatever is far from the average — flags the loud-but-healthy rows and misses the quiet broken ones. The essay explains the one mechanism by its smallest case: an autoencoder is trained to output its own input through a layer narrower than the input, so it cannot copy and must keep only the directions along which the training rows actually vary; step by step, the encoder maps a row to a short code, the decoder maps the code back, the loss is the squared difference between row and reconstruction, and after training that same squared difference is a score that is small for rows shaped like the training data and large for rows that break its pattern — with linear layers and squared error the learned code is exactly the principal-component projection, which is why the two-dimensional case can be drawn. The worked example (invented) takes two standardised features that move together, so the one-number bottleneck learns the line y = x: a row (x, y) is encoded as its position along the line, decoded to ((x+y)/2, (x+y)/2), and its squared error works out to (x − y)²/2, so the six normal rows (1, 1.2), (2, 1.8), (3, 3.1), (−1, −0.9), (0, 0.2), (−2, −2.2) score 0.02, 0.02, 0.005, 0.005, 0.02, 0.02, while (2, −1) scores 4.5 — even though (2, −1) sits 2.24 from the origin and the normal row (3, 3.1) sits 4.31 from it, which is why distance from the average would have flagged the wrong one. The exercise is PyTorch on CPU: generate 500 rows near y = x, train `nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2, 1), nn.Linear(1, 2))` on squared reconstruction error for 500 Adam steps, compare the decoder's direction with the top singular vector of the centred data, and score four probe rows; the expected result (seed 0, run) is a training error of 0.0113, a cosine of 1.0 between the learned and principal directions, and probe errors `[0.019, 0.004, 4.938, 0.0]` for (1, 1.2), (3, 3.1), (2, −1) and (10, 10) — the last line showing the limit the reader must carry away: a row that is extreme along the learned pattern is not anomalous to this score.

## Pitch B — a 95th-percentile threshold flags 5 % of anything (recommended)

Your anomaly detector flags about 5 % of machines every week — in the week the plant was fine and in the week a batch of bearings failed — and the on-call team has stopped reading its alerts, because the threshold was set as a percentile of the very scores it is judging, so the flag rate was chosen when the threshold was written. The essay explains reconstruction error as the label-free score (bottleneck, reconstruct, squared difference, briefly as in Pitch A) and then the step that turns a score into a flag: a threshold computed as the 95th percentile of a batch's own scores flags the top 5 % of that batch by construction, whatever is in it, while a threshold computed once from scores on known-clean validation rows and then frozen makes the flag count rise and fall with the number of rows that reconstruct badly — so the reader learns to fix the threshold on clean data, to report the flag rate as a measured quantity, and to treat a flag rate that never moves as a sign that the threshold is being refitted. The worked example (invented, recomputable with the linear-interpolation percentile rule, position 0.95 × 19 = 18.05 in a sorted list of 20): clean validation errors 0.001, 0.002, …, 0.020 give a frozen threshold of 0.019 + 0.05 × 0.001 = 0.01905; a new all-clean batch of errors 0.0009, 0.0018, …, 0.018 gets a refitted threshold of 0.017145 and one false flag, but zero flags against the frozen threshold; a batch of sixteen normal rows (0.001 … 0.016) plus four broken ones (2.0, 3.0, 4.5, 6.0) gets a refitted threshold of 4.5 + 0.05 × 1.5 = 4.575 and flags only the 6.0 row, while the frozen threshold flags all four. The exercise is PyTorch on CPU (about five seconds): train an 8 → 16 → 2 → 16 → 8 autoencoder on rows that lie near a two-dimensional subspace, freeze the 95th percentile of scores on 1,000 clean validation rows, then score three 1,000-row batches whose anomaly rates are 0 %, 5 % and 20 % (anomalies: random directions with the same norm as normal rows) under both thresholds; the expected result (seed 0, run) is that the refitted threshold flags 0.050 of every batch and its recall falls to 0.25 at 20 % anomalies, while the frozen threshold flags 0.060, 0.100 and 0.249 with recall 1.00 and 0.99.

Why recommended: it carries the catalog's whole mechanism, its failure mode is the one a working engineer will actually ship (the percentile rule is the common recipe), and it ties the essay to the monitoring essays around it — a flag rate is a monitored quantity only if the threshold is not refitted.

## Pitch C — first beat the reconstruction that does nothing

Your autoencoder's training and validation errors both came out at about 1.0, close together, and you read that as a model that fits and does not overfit — but on standardised data an error of 1.0 is exactly what a network earns by outputting zeros, so the “anomaly score” was ranking rows by how loud they are. The essay explains the reconstruction-error score through its baseline: on columns standardised to mean 0 and variance 1, predicting 0 for every value has a mean squared error of exactly 1 (the variance), and a per-row error equal to the row's mean squared value, so a model near that error has learned nothing about which features move together; the bottleneck is worth something only when its error falls well below the do-nothing number, and only then does the score separate a row that is loud from a row that is wrong — with two checks the reader can apply to any detector: compare the validation error with the zero predictor, and make sure the output layer can produce the values it is asked to reconstruct (a sigmoid emits only 0 to 1, and standardised data is half negative). The worked example (invented): one standardised column 1, −1, 1, −1 has mean 0 and variance 1, and predicting 0 gives errors 1, 1, 1, 1, mean 1; with two features that move together, the zero predictor scores the normal row (1, 1) at 1, the broken row (1, −1) also at 1, and the loud but normal row (2, 2) at 4 — top of the list — while a bottleneck that has learned the line y = x scores them 0, 1 and 0 (per-feature mean of (x − y)²/2 over two features). The exercise is PyTorch on CPU (about ten seconds): standardise 6,000 rows that lie near a two-dimensional subspace of eight features, train the same 8 → 2 → 8 autoencoder once with a sigmoid output and once with a linear output, and compare both with a zeros predictor on validation error and on how many of 100 planted anomalies (same norm as normal rows, wrong correlations) land in the top 100 scores out of 1,000; the expected result (seed 0, run) is `predict zeros val MSE 0.9958, 7/100`, `sigmoid output val MSE 0.5735, 13/100`, `linear output val MSE 0.0116, 93/100`.

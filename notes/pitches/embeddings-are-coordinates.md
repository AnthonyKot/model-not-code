# embeddings-are-coordinates — pitches (2026-09-12)

**Working title:** Nearest Neighbour in 384 Dimensions Is the Whole Trick
**Mechanism (catalog):** An encoder maps text to a vector; cosine similarity ranks; contrastive training pulls matching pairs together.

## Pitch A — the search is a dot product over a table you built earlier

You assume "semantic search" means a model reads the documents when the query arrives, so you either run the encoder over the whole catalogue per request or cannot see how a query with none of the document's words can still find it. The essay explains that the model runs once per document at indexing time and once per query at request time, each run producing a fixed-length vector, and that the ranking is arithmetic on those vectors alone: dot product divided by the two lengths gives the cosine of the angle between them, a number in [−1, 1]; the essay derives the formula, shows why dividing by the lengths matters, and shows the whole catalogue scored in one matrix multiplication followed by an argsort. The worked example (invented) is a three-number "encoder": query (1, 2, 2) against documents d1 = (2, 4, 4), d2 = (8, 8, 0) and d3 = (0, 0, 1) gives raw dot products 18, 24 and 2 — d2 first — but lengths 6, 11.31 and 1 give cosines 1.000, 0.707 and 0.667, d1 first, so the long vector that won on dot product loses on angle, every figure recomputable by hand. The exercise uses a stand-in encoder anyone can write — a word-count vector over eight short product titles the essay supplies — with cosine ranking and argsort in twenty lines; the expected result is that titles sharing the query's rarer words rank first, that a title repeating "keyboard" three times does not beat one that says it once under cosine though it does under raw dot product, and that the query "device for typing" matches nothing, which is precisely the gap a learned encoder closes and the reason the next pitch exists.

## Pitch B — "similar" means whatever the training pairs said it meant

Your off-the-shelf embedding model ranks well on English queries and badly on the Spanish ones, or on your domain's jargon, and you cannot say what would fix it because you do not know what the model was trained to do. The essay explains contrastive training as the mechanism: pairs of texts labelled matching or not (the lecture's user query and product title, or word2vec's word and its neighbour against a random word as the ancestor of the idea), the same encoder applied to both sides, and a loss that pushes the cosine of matching pairs up and of non-matching pairs down, so that the geometry of the space is a record of which pairs were shown; the essay then states the consequence plainly — a pair type absent from training gets no pull, which is the lecture's reported finding that a model trained on same-language pairs failed on a Spanish query against an English catalogue. The worked example (invented) is one training step in two dimensions: a fixed query (1, 0), a positive (0.6, 0.8) at cosine 0.6 and a negative (−0.6, 0.8) at cosine −0.6; the gradient of the cosine with respect to the positive is (0.64, −0.48), a step of 0.5 followed by renormalising moves it to (0.854, 0.520) at cosine 0.854, and the mirror-image step moves the negative to (−0.854, 0.520) at cosine −0.854 — one update, both pairs, checkable on paper. The exercise is that update loop in thirty lines run for twenty steps on six two-dimensional points with three labelled pairs and random negatives (expected: matching cosines reach 1.000 and non-matching −1.000 within twenty steps), and then a deliberate omission — leave one pair type out of the labels and watch its cosine not move — after which the reader can look at any model card's description of its training pairs and predict which of their queries it will rank well.

## Pitch C — one pass per document, one per query, and a masked mean in between

You are asked to add "find similar" to a product catalogue, your first design is a model that takes the query and one document and outputs a match score, and it works in the demo and cannot be served, because a pair-scoring model has to run once per document per query. The essay sets the two designs side by side — the pair scorer, and the encoder that turns each text into one vector so documents are encoded once and stored — then explains the step that turns a transformer's per-token outputs into one vector: mean pooling with the attention mask, in which the padding positions are multiplied by zero, the rest are summed, and the sum is divided by the count of real tokens rather than the padded length. The worked example (invented) has five token outputs of width three, (1,2,3), (3,2,1), (2,2,2) and two padding slots the model filled with (9,9,9): the masked mean is (2, 2, 2) and the unmasked mean is (4.8, 4.8, 4.8), so forgetting the mask moves every document vector by an amount that depends on how much padding it received; the essay then counts model passes for an invented catalogue of 1,000 items and 500 queries a day — 500,000 a day for the pair scorer, 1,000 once plus 500 a day for the encoder, with the per-query ranking a 1,000 × 384 by 384 × 1 matrix product of 384,000 multiplications, cheap next to a forward pass. The exercise is masked mean pooling in ten lines checked against the toy (expected: (2, 2, 2), unchanged when the padding values are altered), followed by the pass count for the reader's own catalogue size and query rate; the expected result is the number of encoder runs each design costs per day and the sentence the reader can now say to a colleague about why the vectors are stored and the model is not consulted at ranking time.

Draws on: course-4735368/34-02, course-4735368/34-04, course-6100015/05-04; supporting book: Raschka, *Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)* (`raschka-qai`).

## Note on sources

All five cited lectures carry the material. `course-4735368/34-02` is the core: the pair-scoring versus vector-producing designs with the lecture's pass-count arithmetic (350 million items, 10,000 users, 3.5 trillion passes versus 350 million plus 10,000), Sentence-BERT's siamese structure, mean pooling, the (u, v, |u − v|) head with a sigmoid, cosine similarity derived from the dot product and the angle, and the reported 65 hours → 5 seconds; `34-04` adds masked mean pooling with the attention mask, cosine over a 1.8 million × 384 matrix by matrix multiplication, a hand dot product (1,2,3)·(0,5,−1) = 7, argsort, and the reported failure of a same-language-trained model on Spanish and Japanese queries plus the "multi-qa is not multilingual" correction. `25-01` is word2vec: the V × E embedding matrix, CBOW, skip-gram, negative sampling as pairs labelled 1 or 0, and the transfer of the embedding matrix to a second task — the ancestor of Pitch B's pair training. `course-6100015/05-04` distinguishes autoregressive from encoder models and tokens (inputs) from vectors (outputs) and names BERT, OpenAI's text-embedding-3 and all-MiniLM-L6-v2 as instances; `05-05` gives the points-in-space picture, king − man + woman ≈ queen as reported, and mentions cosine only as a technicality. Neither of the two Ed Donner lectures contains arithmetic; they support the framing sentences, not the worked examples. The "384" in the working title is the reported output width of the lecture's model and stays `reported`; the worked examples above use only the book's own numbers, verified by running the toy computations, and the contrastive loss in Pitch B (cosine of the positive minus cosine of the negative, with renormalising) is the book's own simplification of the lecture's sigmoid-over-concatenation head, to be labelled as such and checked against the supporting book at drafting time.

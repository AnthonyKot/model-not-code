# input-pipeline-is-the-bottleneck — pitches (2026-09-12)

**Working title:** Your GPU Is Waiting on Your JPEG Decoder
**Mechanism (catalog):** Serialise once, read sequentially, map in parallel, prefetch: the producer–consumer pattern applied to training.

## Pitch A — a training step is max(producer, consumer), but only once you overlap them

Your GPU utilisation graph sits at 30% during training and the first proposal on the table is a bigger GPU, which would make the number worse, because the step time is being set by the CPU work that produces each batch. The essay explains the training loop as a producer–consumer pair: the producer decodes, augments and batches, the consumer runs the forward and backward pass; run in sequence a step costs L + C, run overlapped with a prefetch buffer it costs max(L, C), and a parallel map divides L by the number of workers — three arithmetic facts that tell you which knob to turn before you buy anything. The worked example (invented) is a batch of 64 JPEGs at 4 ms each to decode and augment on one core (L = 256 ms) and a GPU step of 80 ms: the reader computes 336 ms per step sequentially (GPU busy 24% of the time), 256 ms with prefetch alone (31%), and 80 ms with four parallel map workers plus prefetch (L = 64 ms, now below C), then the epoch cost for 10,000 images at 157 steps — 52.8 s, 40.2 s, 12.6 s — and sees that the largest gain came from the parallel map, not the prefetch, because L was three times C. The exercise is a thirty-line simulation in any language with threads or async: a producer that sleeps 4 ms per item and a consumer that sleeps 80 ms per batch of 64, run sequentially, with a one-batch prefetch queue, and with four producer workers; the expected result is per-step times near 336, 256 and 80 ms, and a reader who changes the sleeps to their own measured decode and step times gets a forecast for their own pipeline before touching it.

Draws on: course-4735368/03-11 (prefetch as overlapping loading with training, the lecturer's sketch of load-then-train time falling to about two thirds; shuffle, batch, prefetch with an autotuned buffer), course-4735368/11-04 (map with a parallel-calls setting, autotuned, applied to the training set only), course-4735368/11-05 (prefetching large chunks so the model never waits). Supporting book: *Distributed Machine Learning Patterns* (input pipeline and sharding chapters).

## Pitch B — twenty thousand small files is an open() benchmark, not a dataset

Your first epoch on a cloud disk takes minutes before the loss moves, and a profile shows the time is spent opening files rather than decoding them, because the dataset is stored the way it was collected: one small file per example. The essay explains the storage half of the pipeline: pay the per-file cost once by serialising examples into a few large record files, read them as long sequential streams, and split those files into shards so each worker owns a stream — with the lecturer's reported rule of thumb (about ten shards of ten to a hundred megabytes per host, worked in the lecture as forty shards of 250 MB for a 10 GB set on four hosts) as the sizing example, and the option of storing already-decoded or already-augmented examples as the trade the essay weighs (once stored, the augmentation is no longer random per epoch). The worked example (invented) is 20,000 files of 20 KB on a disk with 5 ms per open and 100 MB/s throughput: the reader computes 100 s of opens plus 4 s of transfer per epoch against 40 shards of 10 MB at 0.2 s of opens plus the same 4 s, a 104 s to 4.2 s change with the bytes unchanged, and then computes how many epochs it takes for a one-off 30 s serialisation pass to pay back. The exercise runs on a laptop: write 5,000 files of 20 KB and one 100 MB file with the same bytes, time reading all of each with plain file I/O, then drop the OS cache if you can and time again; the expected result is that the many-file read is several times slower, the ratio grows on a cold cache or a network mount, and the reader now has a per-open latency of their own to plug into the arithmetic.

Draws on: course-4735368/11-05 (small files of a reported ~17 KB versus 10–100 MB records; serialise once, protobuf examples, sharding by host with the reported rule of thumb and arithmetic; storing augmented data or embeddings instead of raw images; prefetching whole shards), course-4735368/03-11 (the pipeline stages downstream of the reader). Supporting book: *Distributed Machine Learning Patterns* (data sharding).

## Pitch C — everything upstream of the first trainable weight is cacheable

You are training a small head on a frozen backbone and every epoch recomputes the backbone's forward pass on the same ten thousand images, so a job that trains a few thousand parameters takes as long as one that trains millions. The essay explains one mechanism, memoising the pure prefix of the pipeline: decoding, resizing and a frozen backbone are all deterministic functions of the stored bytes, so their outputs can be computed once and serialised as the dataset the trainable part reads — the same "serialise once" move as storing decoded images, taken one stage further — with the cost being that any random augmentation must sit before the frozen part and is lost once the outputs are cached, which is the limit the essay works through. The worked example (invented) is a backbone at 20 ms per image and a head at 0.5 ms per image over 10,000 images and 30 epochs: recomputing costs 10,000 × 30 × 20.5 ms = 6,150 s, caching costs one backbone pass of 200 s plus 30 head epochs of 150 s = 350 s, and the cache of 10,000 vectors of 1,280 floats is 51 MB — smaller than the JPEGs it replaces — so the reader can see the break-even at the second epoch and the storage cost before deciding. The exercise is numpy on a CPU: stand in for the backbone with a fixed random 3,072 × 256 matrix multiply plus a 5 ms sleep per image and for the head with a 256 × 10 multiply, then time five epochs recomputing versus caching the 256-vectors; the expected result is that the cached run costs about one backbone pass plus five cheap epochs, and that turning on a random flip before the backbone forces the reader to choose between the cache and the augmentation — the trade the essay named.

Draws on: course-4735368/11-05 (storing the outputs of a fixed model section as embeddings so only the trainable section is trained; storing preprocessed and augmented data once), course-4735368/03-11 (batch and prefetch on the cached dataset). Supporting book: *Distributed Machine Learning Patterns*. (Overlaps the freeze step of `transfer-learning-freeze-then-thaw`; this pitch treats caching as a pipeline mechanism and leaves what freezing does to that essay.)

# labels-arrive-later — pitches (2026-09-13)

**Working title:** What You Can Measure Before the Labels Exist
**Mechanism (catalog):** Prediction time, feedback time and label time are three clocks; each makes a different set of quantities measurable, a short feedback window under-counts, and a cumulative metric hides a dip.
**Absorbs** the feedback-path content of the dropped `design-the-data-loop-first` (prediction → feedback → label → evaluation or retraining); its five-step process survives as at most one paragraph.

## Pitch A — three clocks, and the window that turns silence into a label (recommended)

You shipped a model and the dashboard shows only latency and error rate, the team asks "is it any good?", and the honest answer is "we will know in a month" — but the week's decisions (roll back, retrain, keep) are made on whatever click rate the pipeline computed after a few minutes, and that number is biased low. The essay explains one mechanism, the feedback path drawn against three clocks: (1) at **prediction time** you can measure the service (requests, latency, errors) and everything about inputs and outputs — feature summaries and the score distribution — provided each prediction is logged with an id, a timestamp and the model version; (2) at **feedback time** an event (a click, a dispute, a return) is joined back to the logged prediction by that id, and after a chosen window with no event the prediction is given a *presumed* negative label; (3) at **label time**, once the window for late events has closed (for card fraud, the dispute period), the label is final and precision, recall and the business metric become computable — so the window length is a trade between speed and correctness: a short window yields labels sooner for monitoring and retraining, and every positive that arrives after it is recorded as a negative. The worked example (invented) is 1,000 ad impressions that eventually receive 40 clicks — 30 within five minutes, 6 more within the hour, 4 later — so the click rate is 30/1,000 = 3.0% at a five-minute window, 36/1,000 = 3.6% at one hour and 4.0% when the tail is in: the five-minute figure under-counts by a quarter and 10 of its 970 presumed negatives (1.03%) are wrong; a second model with the same 40 eventual clicks arriving later (20 within five minutes, 32 within the hour) reads 2.0% against 3.0% at five minutes and 3.2% against 3.6% at an hour, so a short window does not just shrink both numbers, it ranks two equally good models by how fast their users click. The exercise is PyTorch on CPU: 10,000 simulated impressions with a 4% click probability, three quarters of clickers delayed by an exponential with a one-minute mean and a quarter by one with a three-hour mean (`Tensor.exponential_` with a seeded generator), and for windows of 5, 60 and 1,440 minutes the click rate seen, the true click rate and the number of presumed negatives that were really clicks; the expected result, from the run, is `ctr seen 0.0315 … true 0.0425 … 110` at 5 minutes, `0.0349 … 76` at 60 minutes and `0.0425 … 0` at a day — and the reader's second step is to write, for a system they run, the three clocks and which of their current dashboard numbers belongs to each.

Draws on: `huyen-dmls` physical pp. 317 (whether a task has natural labels, and when they arrive, as the first monitoring question), 318 (natural labels; feedback-loop length from seconds to weeks; presumed negatives after a window; window length as a speed-versus-accuracy trade; most ad clicks inside five minutes but some hours later, so short windows under-estimate the click-through rate — reported; card disputes over a window of one to three months — reported; long-loop labels suit quarterly reports, not fast detection), 319 (operational expectations versus ML-performance expectations; ML failures are often silent). `ml-system-design` EPUB `OEBPS/Text/chapter-14.xhtml` §14.2 (service usage and performance metrics; log every prediction with timestamps, inputs and outputs). course-6100015/04-18 lines 109–134 (the business outcome is measurable only later and through noise, so a model metric is optimised now and the outcome checked afterwards), course-6100015/06-08 lines 15–28 (productionising includes monitoring and continued evaluation against drift).

## Pitch B — the dip you see depends on the window and on when the labels land

Accuracy on the monitoring page moved from 90% to 87% over a day, nobody was paged, and the post-incident review found two hours at 60% — because the page shows cumulative accuracy, and the labels for those hours arrived six hours after the damage anyway. The essay explains two running statistics against the label clock: (1) sliding accuracy is computed inside one window and reset at the next, cumulative accuracy keeps adding every labelled prediction since the start, so a short bad stretch is diluted by all the good ones before it; (2) any accuracy for hour *h* exists only at hour *h* + label delay, so the earliest possible alert is the dip's start plus the window plus the delay; (3) a smaller window reacts to real dips faster but its accuracy is noisier and trips an alert threshold on healthy traffic more often, so the window is chosen from the false-alarm rate you can tolerate, computed from the binomial count of correct predictions in the window. The worked example (invented) is 100 labelled predictions an hour at 90% accuracy, except hours 16 and 17 at 60%: cumulative accuracy is 90.0% after hour 15, 88.1% after 16, 86.5% after 17 and 87.5% at the end of the day while the sliding value falls 30 points, and with a six-hour label delay the hour-16 figure first exists at hour 22; with an alert at 84% or below, a healthy hourly window (100 predictions) trips with probability 0.0399, about one false alarm a day over 24 windows, while a 25-prediction window trips with probability 0.2364, about 22.7 a day over 96 windows — and both catch a 60% hour almost surely (probabilities 1.0000 and 0.9976). The exercise is PyTorch on CPU: a seeded `torch.rand` stream of correct/incorrect predictions (24 hours × 100, with the two bad hours), sliding accuracy by `mean` over each hour, cumulative accuracy by `cumsum`, each printed with the hour at which it becomes known, then 30 healthy days split into non-overlapping windows with `Tensor.unfold` to count false alarms per window size; the expected result, from the run, is `hour 16: sliding 0.64 cumulative 0.872 (known at hour 22)`, `hour 17: sliding 0.58 cumulative 0.855 (known at hour 23)`, and false alarms per day of `22.07`, `0.93` and `0.00` for windows of 25, 100 and 400 predictions — plus one healthy hour (`hour 14: sliding 0.84`) that would have paged someone at that threshold.

Draws on: `huyen-dmls` physical pp. 331 (accuracy metrics need ground truth, which in production is missing or delayed), 333 (the time-scale window decides which shifts are detectable; a weekly cycle is invisible to a shorter window), 334 (sliding versus cumulative statistics; cumulative accuracy hiding a dip between hours 16 and 18, in a figure credited to MadeWithML), 335 (a shorter window detects sooner but raises false alarms; merging hourly statistics into daily views). `geron-pytorch` physical p. 132 (monitoring code checks live performance at intervals and alerts on drops; performance may fall fast when a component breaks or decay slowly enough to go unnoticed). `ml-system-design` EPUB chapter 14 §14.4.3 (monitoring without ground truth: proxy metrics with carefully set thresholds and alarms). course-6100015/06-08 lines 22–28 (evaluation continues in production).

## Pitch C — before any label: watch the scores, and know what that cannot see

The fraud labels for this month's transactions arrive in one to three months, and you still have to say today whether the model is behaving, so the question is which label-free signal is worth an alert and what it will miss. The essay explains monitoring the prediction distribution as a proxy: (1) the model's scores are one number per request, available at prediction time, so today's scores can be compared with a reference window (the validation run or last month); (2) summary statistics — mean, share above the decision threshold — are the first check but can match while the distribution differs, so a two-sample test on the full distribution follows: the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic is the largest vertical gap between the two empirical cumulative distributions, glossed term by term, and it works on one dimension, which scores are; (3) a moved score distribution says the inputs moved, not that accuracy fell, and an unchanged one does not say accuracy held: if the relationship between inputs and outcome changes while the inputs do not, only labels will show it — so this signal buys time and never replaces the label clock. The worked example (invented) compares eight reference scores (0.05, 0.10, 0.12, 0.20, 0.25, 0.30, 0.40, 0.55) with eight of today's (0.15, 0.22, 0.35, 0.45, 0.50, 0.60, 0.70, 0.80): the reader tabulates both cumulative fractions at each of the 16 values, finds the largest gap is 0.5 (at 0.30, where 6/8 of reference and 2/8 of today's scores are at or below, and again at 0.40), and sets it beside the summary view — mean score 0.246 → 0.471, share above 0.5 from 1/8 to 3/8. The exercise is PyTorch on CPU: a fixed two-feature logistic scorer, 2,000 reference inputs, then two scenarios of 2,000 — inputs shifted by one unit on the first feature, and inputs unchanged but the second feature's effect on the true outcome reversed — with the KS statistic computed from `torch.sort` and `torch.searchsorted`, and accuracy computed only "once labels arrive"; the expected result, from the run, is `inputs moved  KS on scores 0.351 … accuracy once labels arrive 0.767 (ref 0.849)` and `concept changed  KS on scores 0.026 … accuracy once labels arrive 0.772 (ref 0.841)` — two similar accuracy losses, of which only the first was visible on the day it happened.

Draws on: `huyen-dmls` physical pp. 331–332 (when labels are unavailable or too delayed, monitor the input distribution, which needs no labels; label and conditional distributions do; summary statistics as a first but insufficient check; two-sample tests; the KS test is non-parametric and one-dimensional, useful for predictions, and can raise many false positives), 318 (card disputes over one to three months — reported). `ml-system-design` EPUB chapter 14 §14.4.3 (prediction drift and input drift as proxies when ground truth is delayed; model quality metrics once ground truth or feedback exists) and §14.4.4 (with timely labels, rule-based input checks suffice; with delayed labels, drift detection on key features, with a higher threshold for many weak features to limit false positives). `geron-pytorch` physical p. 132 (downstream metrics or human raters on a sample, especially low-confidence predictions, as ways to estimate live performance before labels).

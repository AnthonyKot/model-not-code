# lora-is-a-low-rank-diff — pitches (2026-09-12)

**Working title:** Fine-Tuning Without Touching the Weights
**Mechanism (catalog):** A low-rank delta B·A of rank r is trained beside frozen weights; QLoRA keeps the base in 4-bit and the adapters in higher precision.
**Pilot** — one of three essays that set the book's form; pitches below are written to be drafted as the first Part II essay.

## Pitch A — the adapter is a diff, and a diff of rank r costs (d_in + d_out) × r

You need to change how a three-billion-weight model behaves on your task, a full fine-tune needs every weight plus its gradient and optimiser state in memory — the lecture reports 13 GB just to hold the base at full precision, before training starts — and the only GPU you have is the free one. The essay explains the one trick: leave every weight W frozen and train a separate product B·A whose output is added to W's, where A is r × d_in and B is d_out × r, so the forward pass computes Wx + s·B(Ax), gradients flow only into A and B, and the number of trainable parameters is (d_in + d_out) × r instead of d_in × d_out — the diff is stored beside the program rather than patched into it, which is also why it can be shipped as a small file and merged or removed later. The worked example (invented) is a 512 × 512 attention projection (262,144 weights) with r = 8: A has 4,096 entries and B has 4,096, so 8,192 trainable numbers stand in for a 262,144-entry change (3.1%); over four such projections in each of twelve layers that is 393,216 adapter parameters against 12,582,912 frozen ones, and at four bytes each the adapter file is about 1.57 MB — after which the reader applies the same formula to the lecturer's reported configuration (r = 32 on the four attention projections of 28 layers, dimensions as the lecture gives them) and can check that it lands near the reported 18 million parameters and 73 MB. The exercise is numpy only: freeze a random 64 × 64 matrix W, create A (4 × 64) at random and B (64 × 4) at zero, and fit W + B·A to W + T by gradient descent on A and B alone for two targets T — one built as the outer product of two vectors, one fully random; the expected result is that the loss goes to near zero for the first and stalls well above zero for the second, and the reader has seen both what a rank-4 diff can express and that B starting at zero means training begins exactly at the base model.

Draws on: course-6100015/07-02 (freeze everything; target modules; two matrices A and B multiplied, scaled and added to the target module), course-6100015/07-03 (r as the inner dimension; common values 8, 16, 32 as reported; attention projections as the first targets), course-6100015/07-05 (A as 3000-in 32-out and B as 32-in 3000-out for r = 32 as reported; adapters kept in full precision; the reported 18 million parameters and 73 MB), course-6100015/07-06 (the parameter arithmetic repeated; adapter file size on disk 73.4 MB as reported; the reported heavy configuration of r = 256 on attention and MLP giving 389 million parameters and 1.56 GB), course-6100015/07-04 (the layer dimensions of the base model as reported: 28 decoder layers, inner width 3072, attention projections and MLP gate/up/down). Supporting book: Raschka, *Build a Large Language Model (from scratch)*, LoRA appendix; the LoRA paper (arXiv:2106.09685) for the definition of the scale.

## Pitch B — rank is how much change fits, and alpha is how loud it plays

You copied r = 64 and alpha = 16 from one repository and r = 8 and alpha = 16 from another, got different results, and could not say which of the two numbers did it, because you had no picture of what either one changes in the forward pass. The essay explains the diff's two dials by construction: a rank-1 matrix is one column times one row and a rank-r matrix is the sum of r of those, so r bounds how many independent directions of change the adapter can express; and the product is multiplied by a scale before it is added — the lecture describes alpha as that multiplier and the LoRA paper defines the scale as alpha divided by r, which the essay follows and says so — so alpha sets how large a given B·A appears to the frozen network and, with it, the effective step size of training. The worked example (invented) is a 4 × 4 diff built by hand as the outer product of u = (1, 2, 0, 1) and v = (1, 0, −1, 2): the reader writes out the sixteen entries, sees every row is a multiple of v, adds a second outer product to reach rank 2, and then computes the scale for the two copied settings — 16/8 = 2 and 16/64 = 0.25 — so the identical B·A is applied eight times more strongly in the first configuration than in the second, which is why the "alpha = 2r" rule of thumb the lecturer reports keeps the scale at 2 whatever r is chosen. The exercise is numpy only: take a random 16 × 16 matrix, compute its SVD, rebuild it from the top 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 singular directions and report the fraction of squared error remaining at each rank; the expected result is a curve that falls to zero at rank 16 and is still far from zero at rank 1 or 2 for a random matrix — and the reader then replaces the random matrix with one built from three outer products and watches the error hit zero at rank 3, which is the case LoRA bets on: the change you need is closer to the second matrix than the first.

Draws on: course-6100015/07-02 (A and B multiplied and scaled by a factor called alpha before addition), course-6100015/07-03 (r and alpha as the two hyperparameters; alpha = 2r as the reported rule of thumb and the lecturer's reported experience that other values did slightly worse; which modules to target), course-6100015/07-06 (a reported r = 256 with attention and MLP targets for a much larger dataset, the trade of more capacity for more time), course-6100015/07-11 (r, alpha, target modules, quantisation and dropout as the five adapter hyperparameters; dropout 10% as reported). Supporting book: Raschka, *Build a Large Language Model (from scratch)*; the LoRA paper (arXiv:2106.09685) for the alpha/r scaling, which the essay marks as the source where it differs from the lecture's description.

## Pitch C — the Q is a memory budget: bits for the base, bytes for the diff

Your fine-tune fails to allocate before the first step, because a trainable weight costs far more than a stored one: with a common adaptive optimiser each trainable parameter carries its value, its gradient and two running statistics, and a full fine-tune therefore needs several times the model's own size. The essay explains QLoRA as two budgets kept apart: the frozen base is stored at four bits per weight (dequantised on the fly for each matrix multiply — how those four bits are chosen is the Part IV essay's subject and is only named here), while the small adapters and their optimiser state stay at full precision — so the memory is (base parameters × bits / 8) + (adapter parameters × 16 bytes), and the reader can compute which of the two terms is the one they are fighting. The worked example (invented) is a one-billion-parameter base with 5 million adapter parameters: the base costs 4 GB at 32-bit, 2 GB at 16-bit, 1 GB at 8-bit and 0.5 GB at 4-bit, the adapters plus their optimiser state cost 80 MB whatever the base's precision, and a full fine-tune at 32-bit with the same optimiser would need 16 GB before a single activation is stored — set beside the lecturer's reported footprints for a three-billion base (13 GB at full precision, 3.6 GB at 8-bit, 2.2 GB at 4-bit, and 2.27 GB with the r = 32 adapters loaded). The exercise is a spreadsheet: for base sizes of 1, 3 and 8 billion parameters and adapter sizes of 5, 20 and 400 million, tabulate the weight-memory of full fine-tuning, LoRA on a 16-bit base and QLoRA on a 4-bit base, and mark which cells fit under 15 GB with the activations still to come; the expected result is that only the 4-bit column fits the 3-billion row on the free GPU the lecture uses, that the adapter term only matters once r is pushed to the lecturer's reported "heavy" setting, and that the reader can now explain why the Q is applied to the base and never to the adapters.

Draws on: course-6100015/07-03 (32-bit weights at four bytes each, the reported 13 GB; 8-bit as 256 levels and 4-bit as 16 levels mapped to floating values; only the base model is quantised, not the adapters), course-6100015/07-05 (reported footprints of 3.6 GB at 8-bit and 2.2 GB at 4-bit, 2.27 GB with adapters; the configuration flags — 4-bit, double quantisation, NF4, bf16 compute — as of the course's library versions), course-6100015/07-06 (the reported adapter sizes of 73 MB and 1.56 GB and the recap of 13 → 3.6 → 2.2 GB), course-6100015/07-11 (quantisation as trading base-model precision for room to train), course-6100015/07-12 (Adam keeps momentum state and so costs more memory than plain SGD, as reported; the four steps of a training iteration). Supporting books: Raschka, *Build a Large Language Model (from scratch)*; the quantisation book (`quant-ru`) for the per-parameter byte accounting, page to be located at drafting time.

# measure-retrieval-before-blaming-the-model — pitches (2026-09-13)

**Working title:** Did Retrieval Fail, or Did the Model?
**Mechanism (catalog):** Rank-based metrics over a golden question set score retrieval on its own; the answer is judged separately; keyword checks are proxies.
**Pilot** — the first Part III pilot; every pitch below defines a relevance judgement before any metric and keeps keyword checks as a labelled proxy (source map item 3).

## Pitch A — a metric is only as good as the relevance judgement under it (recommended)

Your retrieval dashboard says MRR 1.0 on the golden set and users still get wrong answers, because the check behind the number asked "does a top-ranked chunk contain the keyword?", and a keyword can sit in a chunk that does not answer the question while the chunk that does answer it uses a different word. The essay explains one mechanism in order: (1) a relevance judgement is a recorded decision, per question and per chunk, made by someone who has read both — "this chunk contains information needed to answer the question" (1) or not (0) — stored as rows of (question id, chunk id, grade) and made once per chunk, not inferred from word overlap; (2) given the retriever's ranked list, reciprocal rank is 1 divided by the position of the first judged-relevant chunk (0 if none is in the top k), MRR is its mean over questions, and hit rate@k is the fraction of questions with at least one judged-relevant chunk in the top k; (3) a keyword check computes the same two formulas with "contains the keyword" substituted for the judgement, so it is a proxy with two failure modes — a false hit (keyword in an irrelevant chunk) and a false miss (relevant chunk paraphrased) — and it is reported under its own name, next to a judged score on at least a sample. The worked example (invented) has five questions with six ranked chunks each: judged first-relevant positions are 1, 2, 4, 2 and none, so reciprocal ranks are 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0 and MRR = 2.25/5 = 0.45, with hit rate@3 = 3/5 = 0.6; the keyword check finds its first hit at 1, 2, 1 (question 3's keyword also appears in an unrelated chunk at rank 1), none (question 4's answer chunk says "prize", not "award") and none, giving reciprocal ranks 1, 0.5, 1, 0, 0, MRR = 0.5 and hit rate@3 = 0.6 — the same hit rate, reached by disagreeing on two questions out of five. The exercise is PyTorch on CPU: eight invented one-sentence chunks, five questions, each with one keyword and a hand-judged relevant chunk id, a bag-of-words vectoriser (word counts, `torch.nn.functional.normalize`), cosine scores by matrix product and `torch.topk(scores, 3)`, then MRR@3 and hit rate@3 computed once from the judgements and once from keyword presence; the expected result, from the run, is `MRR@3 {'judged': 0.7, 'keyword': 1.0} hit@3 {'judged': 0.8, 'keyword': 1.0}`, with the two disagreeing rows flagged — the award question (keyword chunk ranked above the answer chunk: judged RR 0.50, keyword 1.00) and the parental-leave question (keyword "leave" in an HR-portal chunk while the answer chunk says "paid time off" and is not in the top 3: judged 0.00, keyword 1.00) — so the proxy scores this retriever as perfect.

## Pitch B — separate the two failures before you change anything

An answer is wrong and the team argues about whether to swap the model or rework the chunking, because a single end-to-end accuracy number cannot say which half of the pipeline failed, and changing the model when retrieval failed spends a week on the wrong half. The essay explains a two-column log read as a two-by-two table: (1) for every golden question record whether a judged-relevant chunk was in the top k given to the model, and separately whether the answer was judged correct against the reference; (2) wrong answers with a relevant chunk present are generation failures (the evidence was there), wrong answers without one are retrieval failures (no model could ground the answer), and correct answers without one are ungrounded — correct from the model's prior or by luck, not evidence the pipeline works; (3) within retrieval, hit rate@k and MRR together separate "never retrieved" (low hit rate) from "retrieved but ranked low" (hit rate well above MRR), which point at different fixes — recall of the index versus ranking — and only after that split does an answer judge (a person or a model with a fixed rubric) get read. The worked example (invented) has 50 questions: 30 retrieved and correct, 6 retrieved and wrong, 10 missed and wrong, 4 missed and correct, so hit rate@5 = 36/50 = 0.72 and accuracy = 34/50 = 0.68, and of 16 wrong answers 10 (62.5%) are retrieval failures — the best possible generator fixes at most 6; among the 36 hits the first relevant chunk sits at rank 1 for 20, rank 2 for 8, rank 3 for 4 and rank 5 for 4, so MRR = (20 + 8/2 + 4/3 + 4/5)/50 = 26.133/50 ≈ 0.523 and hit rate@1 = 0.40 against hit rate@5 = 0.72, which says ranking is costing as much as recall. The exercise is plain Python (pure counting): twelve logged rows of (question id, first judged-relevant rank in the top 5 or none, answer judged correct), bucketed into the four cells, with hit rate@5, MRR and accuracy printed; the expected result, from the run, is cells of 5 retrieved/correct, 3 retrieved/wrong, 3 missed/wrong and 1 missed/correct, `hit@5 0.667 MRR 0.461 accuracy 0.5`, and `wrong answers: 6 retrieval failures: 3 generation failures: 3` — then the reader reads the one missed-but-correct row and writes down why it is not a success.

## Pitch C — when a question needs two chunks, the first hit is not the score

Your comparative questions ("who has been here longer, A or B?") fail even though MRR is high, because MRR stops at the first relevant chunk and scores a ranking that found half the evidence exactly like one that found all of it. The essay explains the rank metrics that look past the first hit: (1) recall@k is the fraction of a question's judged-relevant chunks that appear in the top k (distinct from hit rate@k, which only asks whether at least one does); (2) discounted cumulative gain adds each relevant chunk's grade divided by log<sub>2</sub>(rank + 1), so a relevant chunk at rank 1 counts 1, at rank 2 about 0.63, at rank 5 about 0.39; (3) nDCG divides that sum by the best achievable sum for the same number of relevant chunks, so 1.0 means "all relevant chunks, in the best order" — defined from first principles and glossed term by term, since a ranking with every relevant chunk at the bottom still has non-zero nDCG, and (4) whether rank order matters at all depends on whether the generator sees all k chunks equally. The worked example (invented) is one question with two relevant chunks a and b and four top-5 rankings: [a, x, y, z, b] gives RR 1, recall@3 0.5, recall@5 1 and nDCG@5 = (1 + 0.387)/(1 + 0.631) = 1.387/1.631 ≈ 0.850; [a, b, x, y, z] gives 1, 1, 1 and nDCG 1.0; [x, a, y, z, w] gives RR 0.5, recall@5 0.5 and nDCG 0.631/1.631 ≈ 0.387; [a, x, y, z, w] gives RR 1 and hit rate 1 while recall@5 is 0.5 and nDCG 1/1.631 ≈ 0.613 — MRR calls the last ranking perfect although half the evidence is missing. The exercise is PyTorch on CPU: a `retrieved` tensor of chunk ids (4 questions × 5 ranks), a `qrels` tensor of judgements, `torch.gather` to turn ids into relevance in rank order, and batched reciprocal rank, hit rate@5, recall@3, recall@5 and nDCG@5 with the discount `1 / torch.log2(ranks + 1)`; the expected result, from the run, is `RR [1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 1.0] mean 0.875`, `recall@5 [1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.5] mean 0.75` and `nDCG@5 [0.85, 1.0, 0.387, 0.613] mean 0.713` — the per-question numbers match the hand computation, and the gap between 0.875 and 0.75 is the missing half of two answers.

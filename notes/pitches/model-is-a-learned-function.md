# model-is-a-learned-function — pitches (2026-09-12)

**Working title:** The Program Is Now a Table of Numbers, and Training Is the Compile Step
**Mechanism (catalog):** Loss plus gradient descent turn a parametrised function into behaviour; the artefact you ship is weights plus preprocessing, not code.

## Pitch A — the diff you cannot read

You are asked to review a change that alters what the system does, and the diff is a binary file of floating-point numbers with no logic in it, so your usual instinct — read the code, find the branch — has nothing to grip. The essay explains the one loop that produced those numbers: a function with free parameters, a loss that scores its output against known answers, the gradient of that loss with respect to each parameter, and the update that moves every parameter a small step against its gradient, repeated until the loss stops falling; it walks the four steps in order, then shows why the loss falls fast at first and slowly later (the gradient shrinks as the fit improves), and why a learning rate that is too large sends the loss up instead of down. The worked example (invented, the book's own numbers) is three points on the line y = 2x, a model y = m·x + c started at m = 0, c = 0, and mean squared error: the starting loss is 56/3 ≈ 18.67, the gradients are −18.67 for m and −8 for c, one step at learning rate 0.05 lands on m ≈ 0.93, c = 0.4, and the loss drops to ≈ 3.76 — every figure on the page, recomputable with a pencil. The exercise is the same loop in about fifteen lines of any language with no library: run 200 steps and watch m settle near 1.97 and the loss fall below 0.001 while c drifts back toward 0; then set the learning rate to 0.5 and watch the loss climb past 300 on the second step and keep growing, which is what "training diverged" means when you meet it in a log.

## Pitch B — the artefact is weights plus preprocessing

You ship "the model" as a weights file, serve it behind a fresh normalisation step computed on live traffic, and get answers that differ from the ones the training run produced, with nothing in the weights changed. The essay explains that a trained linear model is a fixed table: for eight input features it holds nine trainable numbers (eight weights and one bias), plus the mean and spread of each feature that the normaliser learned from the training set and that the lecture's model summary lists as non-trainable — those statistics are part of the program, and recomputing them elsewhere is a code change; it then shows how gradient descent fills the nine trainable slots (loss, gradient, step) and why the normaliser is fitted on the training split only, because fitting it on all the data leaks the held-out rows into training. The worked example (invented) is a two-feature car-price model: training statistics of mean 100 and standard deviation 20 for horsepower and mean 5, standard deviation 2 for age, weights 3 and −1 and a bias of 10, so a car of 120 horsepower and age 3 normalises to (1, −1) and scores 3·1 + (−1)·(−1) + 10 = 14; recompute the statistics on a serving batch whose mean happens to be 120 and 3 and the same car normalises to (0, 0) and scores 10 — identical weights, a different answer. The exercise is to write `predict(weights, stats, x)` as a pure function, serialise `weights` and `stats` together to one JSON file (seven numbers, no code), and add a test that swaps only `stats` and asserts the prediction moves from 14 to 10; the expected result is that the test passes, and that you can now say which numbers in the file were produced by training and which by a pass over the data.

## Pitch C — the loss is the spec

You treat the loss as a progress bar, a number that should go down, and do not notice that its formula is the specification the optimiser is actually meeting, so one bad row in the training data quietly bends the fit and nothing in the metrics tells you why. The essay explains that the gradient, not the loss value, is what moves the weights, and that the loss formula decides how hard each training row pulls: under mean squared error a row with error e pulls with strength proportional to 2e, under mean absolute error every row pulls with the same strength regardless of e, and the Huber loss switches from the first to the second at a threshold δ — the essay derives each of those pulls in one line and then runs the update step from the previous pitch under each. The worked example (invented) is five points, four of them on y = 2x and one outlier at (5, 30) where the line would say 10: under a candidate line y = 2x the single outlier contributes 400 of a squared-error sum of 400 and pulls the slope with strength 40 while the other four pull with 0, so gradient descent on squared error settles at slope 6 and intercept −8, a line through none of the points; under absolute error the same descent settles at slope ≈ 2.01, intercept ≈ −0.02, the line through the four. The exercise is to fit both losses with the same fifteen-line loop, 5,000 steps at learning rate 0.01, and confirm those two endpoints; the expected result is two different programs from the same data, and the realisation that choosing the loss was the design decision.

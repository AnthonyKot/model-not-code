# not-available-at-prediction-time — pitches (2026-09-13)

**Working title:** The Feature That Knew the Answer (catalog placeholder; alternative: *Every Feature Has a Timestamp*)
**Mechanism (catalog):** A feature that uses information absent at prediction time makes the offline score a claim about a different function; split by time and fit every transform on the training split only.
**Boundaries.** `validation-set-is-a-budget` already says, in one paragraph, that a normaliser and a fill value are fitted on the training rows; this essay must not re-teach that case and uses it at most as a one-line callback. `model-is-a-learned-function` owns "fitted preprocessing ships with the weights". This essay owns the prediction-time clock: which information exists at the moment a live prediction is made, and how an offline table quietly contains more.

## Pitch A — every feature is computed as of a moment, and the snapshot is the wrong moment (recommended)

Your churn model scored an AUC near 1 on a held-out split and, once live, flagged almost none of the customers who then left, because each training feature was read from a table extracted weeks after the prediction date, so "days since last login" already contained the churn it was supposed to predict. The essay explains the prediction-time clock: (1) fix the prediction time t<sub>p</sub> for every training row — the moment the live system would have scored it; (2) every feature is allowed only events at or before t<sub>p</sub> (the observation window), the label only events after it (the prediction window); (3) a feature read from a current snapshot, or joined on an entity id without a time condition, is computed as of the extraction date instead, so for past rows it includes the prediction window; (4) the offline score then measures a function of the future, not the function you deploy — detectable as a feature whose correlation with the label is implausibly high, or whose removal collapses the score. The worked example (invented) is six customers scored on day 30, churn meaning no login on days 31–75, the table extracted on day 75: last logins before day 30 are days 28, 25, 20, 22, 12, 26, and the churners are C3, C4 and C6 — as of day 30 "days since last login" is 2, 5, 10, 8, 18, 4 and ranks a churner above a non-churner in 5 of the 9 churner/non-churner pairs (AUC 5/9 = 0.56), while as of day 75 (last logins 74, 71, 20, 22, 68, 26) the same column reads 1, 4, 55, 53, 7, 49 and separates all 9 pairs (AUC 1.0) — the whole gain is the 45 days the live system will never have. The exercise is PyTorch: simulate 4,000 customers' daily logins over 75 days (engagement sets login probability and churn risk; churners stop after day 30), build "days since last login" and "logins in the last 14 days" once as of day 30 and once as of day 75, fit a two-weight logistic regression on a random 80% for each, and score both on the held-out 20% and on a fresh cohort whose features can only be computed as of day 30; the run in `workspace/not-available-at-prediction-time/proto_a.py` prints `snapshot (as of day 75): test AUC 0.996 recall@0.5 1.000 | live AUC 0.638 recall@0.5 0.024` and `as of prediction day 30: test AUC 0.617 recall@0.5 0.128 | live AUC 0.646 recall@0.5 0.109` — the honest model's offline number predicts its live number, the leaky one's does not, and at its own threshold the leaky model finds 2% of live churners.

Draws on: `ds-hard-parts` pp. 139–143 (physical; definition of leakage as training data unavailable at prediction that correlates with the outcome and inflates test performance; the outcome as a feature; a function of the outcome; a timestamp labelled at the wrong end of the period; a join across two sloppily filtered time windows in a churn query; detection by comparing production with test performance) and pp. 147–149 (physical; the scoring stage defines t<sub>p</sub>, the prediction window (t<sub>p</sub>, t<sub>p</sub> + P] and observation window [t<sub>p</sub> − O, t<sub>p</sub>]; the training windows shifted back by P so they mirror scoring; a query enforcing both windows); `huyen-dmls` pp. 163–166 (physical; leakage defined as label information in features that is unavailable at inference; the CT-scanner example; detection by feature–label correlation, ablation, and suspicion of a new feature that improves the score sharply; pairs of features that leak together, start and end date). Lecture support is thin for this pitch: course-4735368/03-09 carries only the normaliser case (lines 110–125, 224–227) and would be cited once, if at all.

## Pitch B — split by the thing you will predict for

Your offline accuracy was 97% and a new patient's scans come back no better than a coin, because the rows were split at random and every test scan had a sibling scan of the same patient, with the same label, in the training set — the evaluation measured recognising a patient already seen, which is the one thing production never asks. The essay explains leakage through correlated rows: (1) name the unit a live prediction is made for — a new patient, a new day, a new store; (2) rows of one unit share a label or a shock (two scans a week apart, all clicks on the day an artist died), so they are not independent draws; (3) a random row split places members of one unit on both sides, and any model able to recognise the unit — a nearest-neighbour lookup most obviously, a large network as well — scores on the test rows by recall; (4) so the split is made on the unit: by patient (group split), by time (train on the first four weeks, validate and test on the fifth), with a gap between them when features arrive with a lag. The worked example (invented) is three patients with two one-number scans each — P1 at 0.9 and 1.1 labelled 1, P2 at 2.0 and 2.2 labelled 0, P3 at 3.0 and 3.1 labelled 1, a labelling with no pattern in the feature — scored by one-nearest-neighbour: holding out one scan of each patient (1.1, 2.2, 3.1) every test scan's nearest training scan is its sibling, at distances 0.2, 0.2 and 0.1, so accuracy is 3/3; holding out patient P2 whole, 2.0's nearest training scan is 1.1 (distance 0.9, label 1) and 2.2's is 3.0 (distance 0.8, label 1), so accuracy is 0/2. The exercise is PyTorch: 400 patients with three scans each, an 8-dimensional per-patient offset of scale 2, scan noise 0.3 and a weak label signal of 0.8 on one coordinate, scored with a one-nearest-neighbour classifier via `torch.cdist` under a random 80/20 row split and an 80/20 patient split; `workspace/not-available-at-prediction-time/proto_b.py` prints `random row split accuracy 0.975` and `patient split accuracy    0.508`, and the reader then replaces patients with days and a per-row offset with a per-day shock to see the time split behave the same way.

Draws on: `huyen-dmls` pp. 163–165 (physical; time-correlated data split randomly; the stock-price and song-recommendation examples; split by time, first four weeks for training and week five for validation and test; duplicates before splitting and oversampling after; group leakage with two scans of one patient); `ml-system-design` ch. 7, §7.2.4 "Time-series validation" (EPUB `OEBPS/Text/chapter-7.xhtml`, page unknown; neighbouring days share information; a gap of a few days between training and test both mimics the feature lag and reduces leakage) and the grouped cross-validation bullet in §7.2 (a whole group goes to one side of the split). No lecture carries group or time splitting; course-4735368/03-09 splits by slicing an already-prepared array in order (lines 152–222) and says nothing about why.

## Pitch C — a statistic of the label is the label

You replaced a merchant id with the fraction of that merchant's transactions that were fraud, the test AUC jumped from 0.76 to 0.92, and the gain vanished on new traffic, because the fraction was computed over all rows, test rows included, so each test row's feature contained its own label — and for a merchant seen once, was its label. The essay explains leakage through a fitted target statistic: (1) target (mean) encoding replaces a category by the average label of the rows in that category; (2) any row that contributes to that average carries its own answer into its feature, diluted by the category's count, undiluted at a count of one; (3) fitted on all rows, it inflates the test score; fitted on the training rows only, the test score is honest but the model, trained on encodings that contain the training labels, trusts the column too much and scores worse than without it; (4) so test rows get statistics from the training split, and training rows get them out of fold — computed from the other folds — which is the same rule as "fit on training only" applied one level down. The worked example (invented) is eight rows: merchant A with labels 1, 1, 0; B with 0, 0; C with 1; D with 0, 1; test rows are C's only row and D's second row: fitted on all eight, C encodes as 1/1 = 1.0 and D as 1/2 = 0.5, so the rule "encoding ≥ 0.5 means fraud" gets both test rows right; fitted on the six training rows (labels 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0), C is unseen and gets the training mean 2/6 = 0.33 and D gets 0/1 = 0.0, and the same rule gets both wrong — the 2/2 was the labels. The exercise is PyTorch: 6,000 rows with one real feature (label drawn from `sigmoid(x − 1)`) and a 3,000-level id with no signal, smoothed mean encoding built with `index_add_`, a logistic regression fitted four ways and scored on a 20% test split; `workspace/not-available-at-prediction-time/proto_c.py` prints test AUCs of 0.756 (real feature only), 0.919 (encoding fitted on all rows), 0.598 (encoding fitted on training rows, in-sample) and 0.757 (out-of-fold on training rows, training statistics for test), with the learned weight on the encoding falling from 2.05 and 2.33 to −0.03 in the honest version — the column was worth nothing, and only the out-of-fold construction says so.

Draws on: `ml-system-design` ch. 11 (EPUB `OEBPS/Text/chapter-11.xhtml`, page unknown; mean target encoding uses the target to build a feature and leaks if implemented carelessly; model predictions and target encoding as features call for nested validation) and ch. 7 §7.3.1 "Nested validation" (access to the test score while fitting anything overfits; an inner split inside each outer split); `huyen-dmls` pp. 164–165 (physical; scaling before splitting and filling missing values with statistics from the test split — the feature-statistic cases this pitch generalises to the label); `ds-hard-parts` pp. 142, 144 (physical; standardising with the complete sample's moments as metadata leakage, and the simulation where it matters only because the series has a trend). course-4735368/03-09 lines 110–125 (normalising with the mean of all data leaks held-out rows) as the familiar starting point.

**Recommended: A** — it is the only pitch whose mechanism is the title's (information that does not yet exist at prediction time), it owns ground no published essay touches, and its exercise shows the failure the reader actually meets: a good offline number followed by a model that finds almost nothing live.

# ppo-clips-the-step — pitches (2026-09-12)

**Working title:** PPO: Reuse the Samples, But Not Too Much
**Mechanism (catalog):** The probability ratio new/old is clipped so a reused batch cannot move the policy too far; the trust region is the motivation, RLHF the application.

## Pitch A — the ratio is the leash, and the clip is where it goes taut

You reused a batch of experience for several optimisation epochs to save on samples, the policy fitted that batch and then chose badly everywhere else, and because the next batch is collected by the policy you now have, it never came back — the lecture calls this policy collapse and it is the failure the clipped objective exists for. The essay explains the objective in three moves the reader can compute: store the old probability of each sampled action, form the ratio ρ = π_new(a|s) / π_old(a|s) on every later pass over the batch, and optimise min(ρ·A, clip(ρ, 1 − ε, 1 + ε)·A) — so a sample with positive advantage stops contributing gradient once its action's probability has grown by the factor 1 + ε, and a sample with negative advantage stops once it has shrunk by 1 − ε, while moves in the "wrong" direction are never clipped because the min keeps the pessimistic estimate. The worked example (invented) is a four-row table at ε = 0.2: (π_old 0.30, π_new 0.60, A = +1) gives ρ = 2, clipped 1.2, objective 1.2 and zero gradient; the same probabilities with A = −1 give min(−2, −1.2) = −2 and a live gradient; (0.50 → 0.35, A = −1) gives ρ = 0.7, clipped 0.8, objective −0.8 and zero gradient; (0.50 → 0.35, A = +1) gives 0.7 and a live gradient — the reader fills the table and sees the clip bite only when the update has already gone the way the advantage wanted, by more than ε. The exercise is numpy: a two-action softmax policy, one fixed batch of fifty samples with invented advantages, 200 gradient epochs on that same batch with (i) plain ρ·A and (ii) the clipped objective at ε = 0.2, tracking the action probability and the largest ratio in the batch; the expected result is that (i) drives the probability toward 1 with ratios in the tens while (ii) stalls once the positive-advantage ratios reach about 1.2, and the reader has measured the leash rather than trusted it.

Draws on: course-4635836/12-01 (online methods discard samples; reusing them overfits and can collapse the policy; the ratio with the reported 30% → 60% = 2 example; clipping to [1 − ε, 1 + ε] with a reported ε of 0.3; the min of clipped and unclipped; the case analysis for positive and negative advantage; the pseudocode's run-then-optimise-for-K-epochs loop), course-4635836/11-02 (a bad step in reinforcement learning produces bad data, unlike supervised learning where the data is fixed), course-4635836/11-01 (sample efficiency and reliability of steps as the two problems). Supporting book: Lapan, *Deep Reinforcement Learning Hands-On* (PPO chapter, for the objective in code).

## Pitch B — the surrogate is trusted only near the old policy, and the clip is a cheap fence

One large policy-gradient step made your agent worse, and it stayed worse, because the estimate of "how good would a nearby policy be" that the step was based on is only accurate close to the policy that collected the data. The essay explains the trust region as an approximation with a radius: the improvement of a new policy over the old one is a sum over states of how much more often the new policy picks positive-advantage actions, weighted by how often the *new* policy visits those states; that visitation is unknowable before the step, so it is replaced by the old policy's, which gives a surrogate that agrees with the true performance in value and slope at the old policy and degrades with distance — the KL divergence measures that distance, the trust-region method bounds it, and PPO replaces the bound with a per-sample cap on the probability ratio that needs no second-order machinery. The worked example (invented) compares two three-action policies, (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) and (0.6, 0.3, 0.1): the reader computes the KL as 0.5 ln(0.5/0.6) + 0 + 0.2 ln(0.2/0.1) ≈ 0.048 nats and the per-action ratios 1.2, 1.0 and 0.5, and finds that a mean-KL bound of 0.05 admits the step whole while a ratio band of [0.8, 1.2] admits the first action's change and refuses the third — two fences around the same policy that do not enclose the same set. The exercise is numpy: for a grid of candidate policies around (0.5, 0.3, 0.2), compute the KL from the old policy and the three ratios, and count how many candidates each rule admits and how many both admit; the expected result is that the ratio rule cuts a box and the KL rule a rounded region, that neither contains the other, and that shrinking ε or δ shrinks both — which is why the essay calls the clip a proxy for a trust region and not a trust region.

Draws on: course-4635836/11-02 (line search: direction, then step size; the cost of a too-large step), course-4635836/11-03 (trust region: a bound around the current policy inside which improvement is guaranteed), course-4635836/11-04 (KL divergence between two policies as a sum over actions, or an integral for continuous actions), course-4635836/11-05 (performance difference in terms of advantage and visitation; replacing the new policy's visitation by the old one's; the surrogate matches in value and gradient at the old policy; a KL penalty gives a lower bound), course-4635836/11-06 (the penalty constant is too small in practice; the constrained form with a maximum, then a mean, KL bound), course-4635836/12-01 (the clipped ratio as the practical replacement). Supporting book: Lapan, *Deep Reinforcement Learning Hands-On* (TRPO and PPO chapters).

## Pitch C — the clip needs a sign, and the sign comes from the advantage estimator

Your PPO run trains, slowly and noisily, with λ left at whatever the template set, and you cannot say whether a given sample is pushing the policy toward its action or away from it, because the number multiplied by the ratio — the advantage — is itself an estimate with a knob. The essay follows the advantage into the objective: the one-step residual r + γV(s') − V(s) is the least noisy and most biased estimate, the full return minus V(s) the least biased and noisiest, generalised advantage estimation blends every horizon with weights (γλ)ˡ, and the sign of the result decides which side of the clip is active for that sample — so λ chooses not only how noisy the update is but, for some samples, its direction; the closing section places the same objective in fine-tuning a language model from preferences (the InstructGPT paper's §3.5, where the "advantage" comes from a reward model and a KL term to the reference policy), as the application, sourced from the paper at drafting. The worked example (invented) is a four-step trajectory with all rewards zero, values V = (0.5, 0.6, 0.3, 0.2) and γ = 0.9: the reader computes the residuals 0.04, −0.33, −0.12 and −0.20, then the first step's advantage at λ = 0 (+0.04), λ = 0.95 (about −0.45) and λ = 1 (−0.50) — the sign flips, so the same sample caps probability increases at one setting and caps decreases at another. The exercise is numpy: implement the GAE recursion for an eight-step trajectory and value list given in the essay, tabulate every Aₜ for λ ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.95, 1}, and mark which change sign; the expected result is at least two sign flips in the given data, and a reader who then plugs in their own value estimates can see how much of their update direction is decided by the critic rather than the environment.

Draws on: course-4635836/13-01 (n-step advantage estimates; bias versus variance; the temporal-difference residual; GAE as the exponentially weighted sum with the special cases λ = 0 and λ = 1), course-4635836/12-01 (ratio times advantage; the positive- and negative-advantage cases of the clip), course-4635836/11-05 (advantage as the quantity that separates two policies' performance). Supporting sources: Lapan, *Deep Reinforcement Learning Hands-On*; Ouyang et al., arXiv:2203.02155 §3.5 (the PPO objective with a KL penalty in RLHF), to be read at drafting — none of the lectures mentions RLHF.

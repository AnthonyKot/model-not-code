# the-model-outputs-a-score — pitches (2026-09-13)

**Working title:** The Model Outputs a Score; You Choose the Threshold
**Mechanism (catalog):** Precision, recall and the ROC curve are functions of a threshold over scores; the threshold is a product decision, not a model property.
**Boundaries.** `class-imbalance-changes-the-loss` owns the confusion matrix's first appearance and class weights, and its closing paragraph already promises this essay ("choosing that cut-off on the scores is a separate decision, and a Part III essay takes it up", with an unweighted model at cut-off 0.046 matching the weighted one's 77 positives). This essay does not re-explain weights; it may cite that result in one sentence as the reason a threshold, not a retrain, is often the cheaper lever. `validation-set-is-a-budget` owns "a threshold picked on validation is a selection"; this essay picks the threshold on validation and scores it on test without re-teaching why.

## Pitch A — a cost ratio is a threshold (recommended)

You were handed a classifier that "is 94% accurate" and a queue of cases scored 0.3, and nobody can tell you whether 0.3 means act, because accuracy counts a missed infection and a false alarm as the same error while the product pays very different prices for them. The essay explains the threshold as the place where a cost preference enters: (1) the model outputs a score per item, and "positive" means score ≥ t; (2) each t yields one confusion matrix, so true positive rate (TPR = TP / all positives), false positive rate (FPR = FP / all negatives) and precision (TP / all flagged) are functions of t; (3) the ROC curve is simply every (FPR, TPR) pair obtained by sliding t from above the highest score to below the lowest; (4) choosing t means minimising C<sub>FN</sub>·FN + C<sub>FP</sub>·FP on validation data, and if the scores are calibrated probabilities the minimum sits at t = C<sub>FP</sub> / (C<sub>FP</sub> + C<sub>FN</sub>), because flagging an item with probability p costs (1 − p)·C<sub>FP</sub> and not flagging it costs p·C<sub>FN</sub>. The worked example (invented) is ten items with scores 0.95, 0.85, 0.80, 0.70, 0.60, 0.45, 0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10 and labels 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0: sliding t gives the ROC points (0, 0), (0, 0.2), (0, 0.4), (0.2, 0.4), (0.2, 0.6), (0.2, 0.8), (0.4, 0.8), (0.4, 1), (0.6, 1), (0.8, 1), (1, 1), and the same scores produce three different decisions — with a miss costing five false alarms the cheapest threshold is t = 0.35 (TP 5, FP 2, cost 2); with equal costs t = 0.525 and t = 0.35 tie at 2 errors; with a false alarm costing five misses it is t = 0.825 (TP 2, FP 0, FN 3, cost 3) — while the model, and its AUC of 21/25 = 0.84, did not change. The exercise is PyTorch: fit a one-layer logistic regression on 4,000 synthetic rows (20% positives, two overlapping Gaussian features), sort the 2,000 validation scores once and get every threshold's TP and FP with `torch.cumsum`, pick the cost-minimising threshold for C<sub>FN</sub> : C<sub>FP</sub> = 5 : 1, then score three thresholds on a separate 2,000-row test set; `workspace/the-model-outputs-a-score/proto_a.py` prints `validation-picked threshold 0.173 formula C_FP/(C_FP+C_FN) 0.167` and test (FN, FP, cost) of `(241, 78, 1283.0)` at 0.5, `(69, 459, 804.0)` at the picked threshold and `(64, 473, 793.0)` at the formula — the default 0.5 costs 60% more than either, and the swept and derived thresholds agree because a logistic model's scores are close to calibrated on data like its training data.

## Pitch B — AUC ranks; it does not choose

You compared two models by AUC, shipped the higher one, and it caught fewer fraud cases at the alert volume operations can actually handle, because AUC averages over every threshold while you operate at exactly one. The essay explains what the area measures and what it hides: (1) AUC equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive outscores a randomly chosen negative — count the correctly ordered positive–negative pairs and divide by the number of pairs; (2) that makes it a property of the ranking alone, unchanged by any threshold and by any monotone rescaling of the scores; (3) two rankings with the same count of correctly ordered pairs can put their errors in different places — one at the top of the list, one at the bottom — so their ROC curves cross; (4) the requirement comes first ("no more than 1% of legitimate transactions flagged", "catch 95% of infections"), it names one point on the curve, and models are compared at that point; when positives are rare, a precision–recall curve shows the difference more plainly than ROC. The worked example (invented) is two models ranking the same four positives and four negatives, listed from highest score down: model X gives P P N P N P N N and model Y gives P N P P P N N N; counting, X orders 4 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 13 of 16 pairs correctly and Y orders 4 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 13, so both have AUC 0.8125 — yet with no false alarms allowed X catches 2 of 4 positives and Y only 1, while to catch all four X must accept 2 false alarms of 4 (FPR 0.5) and Y only 1 (FPR 0.25). The exercise is PyTorch: draw 20,000 negative scores from N(0, 1) and two sets of 20,000 positive scores — model X from N(1.5, 1), model Y from N(1.5·√5, 3), chosen so both give the same AUC — compute AUC by rank-summing with `argsort`, and read each model's TPR at FPR 0.01 and FPR at TPR 0.95 from `quantile`; `workspace/the-model-outputs-a-score/proto_b.py` prints `X AUC 0.856 TPR at FPR 0.01 0.201 FPR at TPR 0.95 0.563` and `Y AUC 0.86 TPR at FPR 0.01 0.633 FPR at TPR 0.95 0.925` — at one operating point Y finds three times as many positives, at the other it raises 1.6 times as many false alarms, and AUC ranks them as equals.

## Pitch C — precision belongs to the traffic, and "positive" belongs to the dataset

Your model had 85% precision on the test set and 5% in production, with the same threshold and the same recall, because the test set was balanced and live traffic is 1% positive — and a colleague's dashboard shows false positives falling while missed cases rise, because the dataset stored the dangerous class as 0. The essay explains the two definitions a threshold silently depends on: (1) TPR is computed within the positives and FPR within the negatives, so neither depends on how many of each there are; (2) precision mixes the two groups, precision = TPR·π / (TPR·π + FPR·(1 − π)) where π is the fraction of positives in the population being scored, so the same model at the same threshold has a different precision in every population; (3) "positive" in every library call means "label 1", whatever the dataset's author chose to encode as 1; (4) so a threshold is set against the live prevalence and the class you care about, which you name before reading any confusion matrix. The worked example (invented) takes TPR 0.9 and FPR 0.05 on 10,000 items: at π = 0.5 there are 4,500 true positives and 250 false positives, precision 4,500/4,750 = 0.947; at π = 0.01 there are 100 positives giving 90 true positives and 9,900 negatives giving 495 false positives, precision 90/585 = 0.154 — the analyst reviewing the alerts now looks at five and a half false alarms for every real case, although nothing about the model changed; then the same confusion matrix is relabelled with the classes swapped to show that "false positive" and "false negative" trade names. The exercise is PyTorch: train a logistic regression on a curated balanced set (2,000 rows each of two Gaussian classes), apply threshold 0.5 to a balanced test set and to a live-like set with 100 positives and 9,900 negatives, then re-encode the live labels with the rare class as 0 and count what a label-1-is-positive confusion matrix calls false positives; `workspace/the-model-outputs-a-score/proto_c.py` prints `balanced test 50%: TPR 0.853 FPR 0.147 precision 0.853  (TP 4265, FP 737)`, `live traffic 1%  : TPR 0.810 FPR 0.145 precision 0.054  (TP 81, FP 1431)` and 11 "false positives" under the swapped encoding that are, checked directly, 11 missed cases out of 100 of the rare class.

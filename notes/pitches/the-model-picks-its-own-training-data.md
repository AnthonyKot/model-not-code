# the-model-picks-its-own-training-data — pitches (2026-09-13)

**Working title:** The Model Picks Its Own Training Data
**Mechanism (catalog):** Only items the model shows receive feedback, so the next model trains on the last model's choices; small ranking gaps grow, and random exposure plus a positional feature break the loop.
**Placement:** Part III, after `labels-arrive-later` (it uses that essay's natural labels and feedback window without re-explaining them).

## Pitch A — the gap grows because the top slot is the one people see

Your recommender retrains every night on yesterday's clicks, the same few items have held the top of every list for months, and the offline metrics look excellent — because the clicks it learns from were mostly collected in the slots it chose, so the logs cannot tell a good item from a well-placed one. The essay explains the loop as four steps: the model ranks items; users see the top slots far more often than the lower ones; clicks accrue in proportion to being seen as well as to being good; the next model is trained on those clicks and ranks the clicked items higher still — so any initial difference, including a coin-flip tie-break, is amplified round after round, and the evidence that would correct it is never collected. The worked example (invented, expected values) has two equally good items that each earn a click from 10 % of the users who look at them, a top slot every user looks at and a second slot half of them look at, 1,000 sessions a round, and a ranking by clicks collected so far starting from 11 against 10: after round 1 the counts are 111 and 60, then 211 and 110, 311 and 160, 411 and 210, 511 and 260 — the gap grows from 1 to 251 and the leader's share settles near two-thirds (0.663 after five rounds), and a model that divides clicks by impressions would rate the leader at 0.10 and the other at 0.05, “twice as good”, when the two are identical. The exercise is plain Python (the loop is counting; under a second): five items, slot k seen by 1 user in k, 20 rounds of 1,000 sessions ranked by collected clicks, 20 seeds, then the same with one item 20 % better; the expected result (run) is a mean click share by final rank of `[0.437, 0.22, 0.146, 0.112, 0.086]` for identical items, the first round's random leader still on top in `20 of 20` seeds, and the better item on top at the end in `7 of 20` seeds — every one of them a seed where it started on top.

Draws on: `huyen-dmls` p. 323 (degenerate feedback loop defined: a system's outputs create or process its own future inputs; common where labels come from users, as in recommenders and click-through prediction), p. 324 (two songs with a marginal initial difference, the higher one clicked more and ranked higher still; the names exposure bias, popularity bias, filter bubbles; detection by popularity diversity and by hit rate per popularity bucket; outputs becoming more homogeneous over time).

## Pitch B — an item that is never shown has no score, only a default

You launched with a hand-picked default in the single recommendation slot, and six months later a better candidate that was added the week after launch has not accumulated a single impression, so there is no number anywhere saying it is better — its absence from the training data is read by the next model as “nobody clicked it”. The essay explains the loop at its sharpest, one slot: the model's choice decides which item receives impressions, only shown items receive clicks or presumed negatives, the next estimate is built from those, so an unshown item's quality is not estimated badly but not estimated at all; the fix is to reserve a small, random slice of traffic whose choice ignores the model, which buys an unbiased estimate for every item at a price that can be computed in advance. The worked example (invented, expected values) has item A at a 10 % click rate on 1,000 sessions a round and item B at 12 % never shown: with no exploration A collects 100 clicks and B stays at zero impressions forever; with 10 % of sessions choosing uniformly between the two, A is shown 900 + 50 = 950 times for 95 clicks (0.100) and B 50 times for 6 clicks (0.120), so B takes the slot next round and the round earns 0.12 × 950 + 0.10 × 50 = 119 clicks instead of 0.10 × 950 + 0.12 × 50 = 101 — while, had B been worse at 8 %, the exploration slice would have cost 50 × (0.10 − 0.08) = 1 click per 1,000 sessions. The exercise is plain Python (under a second): one slot, items at 10 %, 12 % and 8 %, A starting with 1 click in 10 impressions, 10 rounds of 1,000 sessions choosing the best observed click rate, 100 seeds, with exploration rates 0 and 0.1; the expected result (run) is `eps=0.0: B on top at the end in 0/100, B never shown in 100/100, mean clicks 998 of 10000 sessions` and `eps=0.1: B on top at the end in 98/100, B never shown in 0/100, mean clicks 1130 of 10000 sessions`.

Draws on: `huyen-dmls` p. 323 (definition), p. 324 (hard to detect offline because the loop needs users; correction by showing users random items and using their feedback to determine true quality), p. 325 (each new item given a random initial pool of traffic to measure unbiased quality before promotion; randomisation improves diversity at a cost in user experience; bandit-style exploration as the more careful version, named only).

## Pitch C — position and quality are one column until something moves the position (recommended)

You added “was shown in the top slot” as a feature so the model could discount position, retrained on the logs, and the model still says the top item is the better one — because in logs where the model always put item A on top, “is item A” and “was on top” are the same column, and no training procedure can divide one effect between two identical columns. The essay explains the loop and both fixes as one mechanism: the model's ranking decides where each item is shown; a click is the product of being seen (which depends on position) and being liked (which depends on the item); a positional feature lets the next model learn the first factor separately, and at prediction time the position feature is set to the same value for every candidate so items are compared on the second factor alone — but the split is only learnable if the logs contain the same item in different positions, which is exactly what a small random-exposure slice supplies. The worked example (invented, expected values): two equally good items, 10 % click rate on top and 5 % in second place; 1,000 exploit sessions log A on top with 100 clicks and B second with 50, so “A” and “top” coincide and the 2 : 1 ratio can be credited to either; add 200 sessions in random order, and the logs now hold A on top 100 times (10 clicks), A second 100 times (5), B on top 100 times (10), B second 100 times (5) — within the random slice each item's rate is 0.10 on top and 0.05 below, the item difference is zero and the position effect is the whole 2 : 1, a gap in log-odds of ln(0.10/0.90) − ln(0.05/0.95) = −2.197 − (−2.944) = 0.747. The exercise is PyTorch on CPU (about six seconds): simulate 20,000 two-slot sessions with equal items and a position that halves the click rate, once with no exploration and once with 10 % of sessions in random order, fit a logistic regression on [is A, is B, on top] with `BCEWithLogitsLoss` and Adam, and print the learned item gap and position weight; the expected result (seed 0, run) is `explore=0.0: item A - item B = +1.242, top position = -0.473` — an item gap that does not exist and a position weight of the wrong sign, because any split fits the confounded logs — and `explore=0.1: item A - item B = +0.012, top position = +0.699`, against the true `+0.000` and `0.747`.

Why recommended: it delivers the catalog's whole mechanism in one argument (the loop, why it is invisible in the logs, and why the positional feature and random exposure are halves of one fix rather than two alternatives), the exercise is real PyTorch training whose failure the reader can see in two printed numbers, and it is the version a senior engineer is most likely to get wrong after reading only the usual advice. Pitch A is the gentler entry if the author prefers the loop without the regression.

Draws on: `huyen-dmls` p. 323 (definition), p. 324 (songs example; detection by hit rate per popularity bucket; randomisation as the first correction), p. 325 (random initial traffic pool and its cost; position bias in a five-song list — is the model good at the top pick or do users click whatever is on top; positional features, numeric or boolean; adding “recommended first” to the training data so the model learns how much being first matters).

# transfer-learning-freeze-then-thaw — pitches (2026-09-12)

**Working title:** Borrow the Eyes, Retrain the Judgement
**Mechanism (catalog):** Freeze a pretrained backbone, train a new head, then unfreeze with a small learning rate.

## Pitch A — the update is learning rate times gradient, and a random head sends a large gradient

You unfroze the backbone to "squeeze out a bit more", kept the learning rate you had used for the head, and validation accuracy fell from around 70% to a third of that in a few epochs — the lecture reports exactly this collapse — because a freshly initialised head pushes large, uninformative gradients back into weights that took days of pretraining to reach. The essay explains the mechanism at the level of one weight: each step changes it by learning rate × gradient, freezing sets that change to zero for the backbone, and unfreezing later with a learning rate a hundred times smaller bounds how far any pretrained weight can drift per step — so the order (freeze, train the head until its gradients are small, then thaw with a small rate) is not a ritual but a way of keeping the product η·g small at every stage. The worked example (invented) is a head on a 1,280-feature backbone output — dense 1,024, batch norm, dense 128, dense 3 — whose trainable count the reader recomputes as about 1.45 million against a frozen backbone the essay sets at 17.6 million (a reported figure from the lecture, used as the scale), followed by the drift arithmetic: a backbone weight of size 0.05 seeing a gradient of 0.5 moves 0.0005 per step at η = 10⁻³ and is overwritten within 100 steps (less than one epoch of 157 batches), while at η = 10⁻⁵ the same erasure takes 10,000 steps. The exercise is numpy only: fit a logistic head on top of a fixed random projection of a toy two-class dataset, then unfreeze the projection at the head's learning rate and at a hundredth of it, and record the accuracy curve for each; the expected result is that the full-rate run drops below the frozen baseline within a few hundred steps and the small-rate run stays at or slightly above it, reproducing the shape the lecture reports (about 70% frozen, a collapse at the same rate, 72.2% at a hundredth of it) without a GPU.

Draws on: course-4735368/13-01 (feature extractor versus classifier; freezing by making the backbone untrainable; reported parameter split of 19 million total, 1.9 million trainable; fine-tuning from the top layers with a very small learning rate), course-4735368/13-02 (transfer first, then fine-tune; the reported drop to 33% at the unchanged learning rate and 72.2% after dividing it by 100; recompiling after changing what is trainable, as of the course's framework). Supporting books: Géron, *Hands-On Machine Learning* (PyTorch edition), transfer learning; Raschka, *Build a Large Language Model (from scratch)* for the same freeze-then-thaw move on a transformer.

## Pitch B — "frozen" stops the gradient, not the statistics

You froze the backbone, trained the head, and still got different validation numbers between two runs on the same data, and when you unfroze, accuracy fell even at a small learning rate — because a batch-norm layer carries two kinds of state and freezing only stops one of them. The essay explains what a layer's state is: parameters, which change only through the gradient, and running statistics (batch norm's mean and variance), which change on every forward pass in training mode regardless of what is trainable; the recipe is therefore freeze *and* run the backbone's normalisation in inference mode, train the head, then thaw the weights while keeping the statistics fixed — with the lecture's careful distinction between "trainable" and "training" as the source, and the batch-norm algorithm from the ResNet lecture as the arithmetic. The worked example (invented) is one batch-norm unit with running mean 0 and variance 1 from pretraining, fed a batch from the new domain with mean 2.0 and standard deviation 0.5: the input 2.5 normalises to 1.0 in training mode and to 2.5 in inference mode, so every downstream frozen weight sees a different number depending on a flag; then with momentum 0.99 the reader computes the running mean after 10, 100 and 200 batches (0.19, 1.27, 1.73) and sees the "frozen" backbone's internal inputs drifting for hundreds of steps. The exercise is fifteen lines of numpy: implement batch norm with running statistics and momentum, feed 200 batches whose mean is 2, print the running mean at 10, 100 and 200 batches and the normalised value of a fixed probe input in both modes; the expected result matches the 1 − 0.99ⁿ curve and shows the probe's output in training mode differing from inference mode until the statistics converge, which is the drift the reader saw between runs.

Draws on: course-4735368/13-02 (trainable versus training; gamma and beta as trainable, mean and variance as adapted statistics; keeping batch norm in inference mode during fine-tuning; dropout as the other layer with a mode), course-4735368/12-03 (the batch-norm algorithm: mini-batch mean and variance, epsilon, gamma and beta, and the argument that gamma and beta can undo the normalisation), course-4735368/13-01 (the head built on the frozen backbone). Supporting book: Géron, *Hands-On Machine Learning* (PyTorch edition), batch normalisation and transfer learning.

## Pitch C — where you cut decides how many parameters your data has to pay for

You have 320 labelled images, a colleague says "just fine-tune the whole thing", and the model trained from scratch on those images cannot cross 50% validation accuracy while it memorises the training set — the lecture reports this pair of outcomes — so the question is not whether to reuse a pretrained network but how much of it to reopen. The essay explains the cut as a decision about parameter counts: early layers compute generic features and late layers task-specific ones, the head is a small classifier over the backbone's feature vector, and thawing "from the top" means each step down the network adds that layer's parameters to what your dataset must constrain — so the essay gives the reader the formula for a convolution layer's parameter count (kernel × kernel × in-channels × out-channels, plus a bias per output channel) and uses it to price each depth of thaw. The worked example (invented) is a four-block convnet — 3 × 3 convolutions with 3 → 32 → 64 → 128 → 256 channels and a three-way head — whose per-layer counts the reader recomputes (864, 18,432, 73,728, 294,912 and 771), giving trainable totals of 771 for head only, 295,683 with the last block thawed, 369,411 with two, and 388,707 for everything, set against datasets of 320, 3,200 and 32,000 images; the reported lecture outcomes (frozen head 71.3%, then 72.2% after a small-rate thaw, from-scratch below 50%) are placed in that table as the lecturer's, not the book's. The exercise is paper and a calculator: take the layer table of any small pretrained convnet the reader can see a summary of (or the one in the essay), compute the trainable count for each depth of thaw and its ratio to their own dataset size, and pick a depth; the expected result is a short table in which the ratio for "thaw everything" at a few hundred images is in the thousands of parameters per example, and a written rule for when the reader would go one block deeper — with the lecturer's reported caution, that a large dataset may train better from scratch, as the counterexample.

Draws on: course-4735368/13-01 (low-level to high-level features; replacing the head; fine-tuning from the final layers backwards; the reported 320-image comparison and the caution about large datasets), course-4735368/13-02 (the reported gain from a small-rate thaw), course-4735368/12-05 (parameter count of a convolution as kernel × kernel × input channels × output channels, with the depthwise-separable variant as the reported 8–9× saving), course-4735368/12-03 (global average pooling as the feature vector the head reads). Supporting books: Géron, *Hands-On Machine Learning* (PyTorch edition); Raschka, *Build a Large Language Model (from scratch)*, fine-tuning for classification.
